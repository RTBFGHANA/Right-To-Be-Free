What forms of child trafficking exist in Ghana today?

Gold mining industry

Ghana is Africa's largest gold producer and the sixth-largest in the world. About one third of the nation’s gold comes from artisanal and small-scale mines, locally called galamsey ("gather and sell"). Most of these mines operate illegally without a license, with simple machinery and limited investment. Therefore, they rely on a large workforce where child labor is common, despite both Ghanaian and international law prohibiting hazardous child labor.

Young boys and girls work up to 14 hours a day, both above and underground. In the tunnels and mine shafts, they risk death from explosions, rock falls, and tunnel collapse. They breathe air filled with dust and sometimes toxic gases. Above ground, children dig, crush, mill, and haul heavy loads of ore. They stand for hours in water, digging sand or silt from riverbeds and then carrying bags of mud on their heads or backs to sieving and washing sites. Finally, they work with mercury by amalgamating it with gold and then burning the amalgam to separate out the gold. Mercury is a highly toxic metal and can be absorbed through the skin or inhaled through mercury vapor. It attacks the central nervous system and can cause lifelong disability, including brain damage, and even death.

Like adults, children suffer the effects of poor ventilation and lighting, exhaustion, and overexertion. But children are particularly vulnerable to exposure to dust and chemicals because their young bodies are still developing. The impact of these dangerous conditions is devastating to their health, causing serious respiratory conditions (e.g. silicosis), constant headaches, hearing and vision problems, joint disorders, and long-term spine and brain damage.

While many of the children are fortunate to attend school, they often miss school due to chronic illness, exhaustion, or their work schedule prevents regular attendance. Many children work in order to raise money for school. For example, 17-year-old Grace carries heavy loads of mud ore to pay the $250 admission cost of secondary school. Although public primary and junior high schools are officially free in Ghana, they charge fees for books, school supplies, parent-teacher association (PTA) dues, and other items. Amma, 16, has worked in gold mining since she was 14 because her mother does not have enough money for school. Sometimes she earns $15 -18 in three weeks, together with her mother. A 12-year-old boy named Fynn said he worked to earn money for a school uniform. These children risk their health and lives in order to afford and attend school.

Child scavengers

Behind the towering hills of waste at the Adepa landfill in Accra, which spans 216 acres, children scavenge through piles of garbage. Their story is one of resilience, hope, and the fervent desire for a better life.

The Adepa landfill attracts people from all over Ghana as a prime location to scavenge. The scavengers residing there often engage in unscrupulous activities such as theft, drug use, etc., creating a perilous environment for children. Anyone working there is at risk of infection from diseases and exposure to toxic chemicals and human waste. Eric Peasah said the stench was intolerable and he would have to take breaks to get some fresh air.

Recently, Eric Peasah made several trips to the Adepa landfill with Barnabas, a young man who had been rescued from the same site some years before. Barnabas said drug users work at the landfill, so marijuana, tramadol, and cocaine are frequently used. He said there are always fights with adults who want to cheat the youths. One time a man stole his money, and he fought until he was injured. Another time he was pricked by a syringe and cut by a broken bottle. Catching a fever and malaria are common illnesses at the dump.

Many children from the impoverished Adepa community, where the landfill resides, depend on this hazardous livelihood in order to make money to support their families. For some, this is a life they were born into since scavenging is their parents' only source of income. They have never attended school; their entire life revolves around living and working at the landfill.

Children navigate through this hazardous environment, looking for pieces of copper, aluminum, etc. from electronic waste (e-waste). Ghana and other developing countries have become the de facto dumping ground for discarded electronics from the U.S. and Europe.

Heavy metals leach from landfills and contaminate water supplies, and when burned, they release toxic particles which are easily inhaled by children, putting them at grave risk for respiratory illness, brain and kidney damage, and eventually cancer. Because of their rapidly developing organs, they are more vulnerable to any poison, contaminant or toxin. According to Nswam-Adoagyiri, Health Director of the Municipal Assembly, the lifespan of children who frequent a landfill is reduced by 20 years.

The minimum daily wage in Ghana is $1.59 per day. In the informal sectors, it is even lower. Unless these children receive an education and the cycle of poverty is broken, they will become adult scavengers. Working at the landfill, they are also vulnerable to traffickers who prey on destitute parents who want a better life for their children.

Fishing industry

Lake Volta, the world's largest man-made lake by surface area, is where children are trafficked to work in the dangerous fishing trade. The children, some as young as four years old, start work in the pre-dawn hours. They cast nets into the murky lake waters in hopes of catching fish. This has become a greater challenge over the years as the lake is overfished. Consequently, fishermen need more child laborers in order to bring in enough fish. Some unscrupulous fishermen have resorted to banned practices, including the use of DDT (dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane), a colorless, tasteless, and almost odorless insecticide. These fishermen illegally dump DDT into the lake, which kills the fish in the vicinity, and children harvest the dead fish, exposing them to this toxic pesticide. DDT was banned in the U.S. in 1972 but is still legal in developing countries.

The children work 14-hour days, seven days a week. They begin working before dawn, laboring for several hours before eating a small meal, the only one they are allowed to eat each day. The smallest error, or failure to meet their master's approval, is punished with a beating.

Masters sometimes taunt the children, telling them their mothers sold them. The enslaved children do not realize their parent(s) were deceived. The masters falsely promise their child will attend school and learn to fish, and that they can help the fisherman during weekends and school breaks. The children are too young to comprehend the deceit that led to their trafficking. All they know is that they are enslaved and have no hope for rescue, because they are told their families gave them to the fishermen. One child said he did not think his family liked him.

This is why psychological counseling is a critical part of the rehabilitation process. Trafficked children shut down their emotions and become stoic, resigned to their hopeless fate. Once they are given their freedom, we help them learn to express their emotions again in a safe environment.

Some of the fishermen were once trafficked themselves - the abused become the abuser - as this is the only life they have known. The cycle continues unless it can be broken through education and reforms.

Eric Peasah works with the village chiefs to gain their respect and trust. This supports his efforts to convince the fishermen to release the child laborers. He speaks to villagers and educates them about child trafficking and explains the value of working without children, so they can pursue an education. He helps the fishermen create co-ops, where they can build a fish pond within Lake Volta and, with microloans, stock the pond with baby fish. The fishermen are also given the opportunity to learn another vocation, such as carpentry or farming. By working with these "receiving" villages, Eric helps end the demand for child labor.

Eric also educates "sending" villages where children are trafficked from. By teaching families about the deceitful strategies traffickers use and the abusive conditions the children endure, villagers now report traffickers who approach their villages seeking children. Working with other non-governmental agencies, Eric helps villagers become financially independent by helping them create private enterprise opportunities to alleviate the poverty that makes them vulnerable to traffickers. These community outreach programs are effective in stopping child trafficking.

Sex trafficking of Nigerian girls to Ghana

The threat of child sex trafficking has seen a horrific increase over the last few years. It is reported by the ILO that globally, over 6.3 million people were trafficked for commercial sex work in 2021. The figure stood at 4.3 million in 2016 (ILO, 2017). Out of these victims, 1.7 million were children (ILO, 2017). In recent years, the northern region of Ghana, specifically the region’s capital, Tamale, has experienced a rise in the number of Nigerian girls involved in commercial sexual activities (Mohammed, A.-R. et al., 2026). Since 2020, there have been over 150 human traffickers convicted and the rescue of thousands of victims (Vincent 2024).

These girls for the most part, were aided by agents in the business who helped them to travel into Ghana to find work. Many of the girls are sent to be hosted by Madams in Ghana via the recruiters in Nigeria. While the majority of girls are recruited this way, there are also some cases of those recruited via social media influencers (Mohammed, A.-R. et al. 2026). It has often been found that in the context of Nigerian trafficking networks that the madam is usually an older woman who was formerly enslaved herself and has repayed her depts and now has become an accomplice (Mancuso, 2013; van der Watt & Kruger, 2017).

A key reason for this risky move is attributable to poverty levels in Nigeria; taking these jobs is often a way for the girls to try to relieve some of the financial burden from their families. There is also a higher risk of being trafficked if one has family and individual risk factors, many victims home a single parent house hold where a parent had either passed or abandoned the family. Beyond this there have been more extreme cases of this where the girls experience homelessness or are runaways and therefore experience an increased level of vulnerability (Mohammed, A.-R. et al. 2026).
