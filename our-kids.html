Senyo’s story

Senyo and his two older brothers were trafficked to two fishermen to work on Lake Volta. Senyo's father was deceased, and his mentally ill mother was unable to care for them. His maternal grandmother was taking care of Senyo, his siblings, and his cousin. They lived in Adafianu, in the Volta Region, near the beach in a coconut palm shack, which provided inadequate shelter, especially when it rained. Because of her impoverished condition, the grandmother struggled to survive, so she gave Senyo to a fisherman and his two brothers. The fishermen promised they would care for the boys and enroll them in school, and they could help them fish on Lake Volta. Senyo was about 12 years old when he was rescued in 2014, although he appeared younger due to malnutrition and his physical disability.

Senyo and his brothers were healthy when they were trafficked to work on Hanikpo, an island on Lake Volta near Kpando-Torkor. They worked long hours bailing water from fishing boats (canoes), mending nets, and diving into the water to disentangle nets that were caught on submerged tree trunks.

The canoe on which the children work is often slippery because spirogyra, a freshwater algae, covers the wood's surface. One day, Senyo slipped and fell, hitting his chest against the canoe seat, fracturing several bones in his chest and back.

The severe injury led to Senyo’s complete incontinence and inability to walk unassisted. He experienced excruciating pain, but was never taken to a hospital for treatment. Instead, Senyo’s master and wife used hot compresses on his injury, but to no avail. According to Senyo, he was still forced to work on the lake. His master later took him to see an herbalist for treatment, and he left him with this stranger. Two years dragged by as Senyo remained with the herbalist and was further traumatized. He was starved, beaten, and forced to crawl to the herbalist’s farm to sweep the large compound. When he soiled himself, he was beaten and forced to clean up. And unbelievably, the herbalist forced him to dance to music.

Fortunately, the intervention of a concerned neighbor led the Department of Social Welfare and Eric Peasah of RTBF/Africa to rescue Senyo. RTBF took him to the FOCOS Orthopedic Hospital in Accra for evaluation. After a series of tests, Dr. Amatefe Mawuli Kwami diagnosed Senyo with five crushed vertebrae (T6-T10), which were compressing his spinal cord. Funds were raised for his life-saving spine reconstruction surgery in 2015.

Senyo’s traumatic childhood left deep psychological and physical scars, but we have been providing him with the care he needs to overcome his feelings of abandonment and distrust of others. After his surgery, he went to live at the Orthopedic Training Center in Nsawam, Ghana. Senyo wants to become a police officer so he can arrest his former master and others that traffick children.

Senyo has overcome the physical and emotional traumas he endured and is thriving and excelling in school. His teachers say he works very hard, and they are amazed at his performance, especially since he never attended school until starting second grade at the age of 16 in 2018. He is quite short for his age, partly due to the hard labor he was forced to perform, which created microfractures (which often stunt the growth of trafficked children), and his spinal injury.

Our kids today

Story of Akwasi

“I want to become a doctor and save lives”

Rescued at age 11, now 22 and a Bishop Herman College Graduate, Science Student.

Akwasi’s childhood didn’t start with school.

At just 9 years old, he was sold by his father to work on the Volta Lake. Instead of learning to read, he was diving into dangerous water to free fishing nets. In 2014, Right To Be Free rescued Akwasi and gave him safety, medical care, and something he had never had before: “a chance at school.”

12 years later, Akwasi just wrote his 2026 WASSCE at Bishop Herman College. He chose General Science because he has one dream: To become a doctor and heal people. “I was given up on as a child. Now I want to give back by saving others.” Akwasi.

STORY OF KOJO

Kojo was just 5 years old when our field officer found him working on the Volta Lake in 2023. A field officer on a scouting mission, concerned for his safety and age, immediately reported the case to the Director, Mr. Eric Peasah. In 2024, after careful arrangements with community leaders and the Department of Social Welfare, our team, led by the Director himself, went back to rescue Kojo.

What we found broke our hearts. Kojo had visible scars and marks on his head and body, and although he would not admit it, his neighbour informed us that they were from severe beatings.

He was sent to a rehabilitation center where he received care, counseling, and support for several months. During this time, his parents also received counseling. After rehabilitation, Kojo was reunited with his mother and reintegrated into his community. To ensure he never returns to the lake, we enrolled him at Amen Preparatory School and provided him with full school support.

Today, Kojo is 8 years old and in Class 1. His teachers describe him as very respectful and hardworking in class. To help the family stay sustainable, his mother, Ama Atta, received microcredit support to grow her business.

Story of Akua

“Education gave me back my voice”

Akua was rescued in 2015 and is now 19 and an Ada Senior High Technical School Graduate.

Akua was rescued in 2015 after being forced into hard labor. Like many girls, she was denied school and basic care. For the last 10 years, Mr. Eric Peasah and Right To Be Free have sponsored Akua’s education. She attended Ada Senior High Technical School and just completed her West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE).

Akua is smart, confident, and wants to study further to become financially independent and help other girls.

“Because someone sponsored me, I am here today. I want to do the same for others one day.”

STORY OF KAFU

“I finished school. I’m ready to build my future.”

Kafu was rescued from Volta Lake as a child and is now 25 and a St. Paul’s Secondary School Graduate.

Kafu was trafficked to Volta Lake as a child and forced to fish for long hours with no pay and no school.

Right To Be Free rescued him and enrolled him in school. With counseling, uniforms, and support, Kafu stayed focused. In June 2026, he graduated from St. Paul’s Secondary School. He is waiting for his results and planning for the future.

Kafu is hardworking, disciplined, and wants to use his education to support his community.

“Right To Be Free gave me hope. Now I want to make my rescuers proud.” Kafu.

Story of Matthew

Matthew’s parents were very poor and struggled to provide for him. A fisherman approached them with the promise of enrolling Matthew in school and when he was older, he could help him fish on Lake Volta, Ghana, during school breaks. They reluctantly agreed so he could have an education they could not afford. Matthew was only four years old and he spent the next 10 years working for the fisherman. He never attended school and worked 14 hour days, enduring years of physical and emotional abuse.

RTBF rescued Matthew in 2014 when he was 14 years old. After going through our six-month rehabilitation program with other rescued children, a home assessment concluded the living conditions were not conducive for Matthew to attend school in his village.

We placed Matthew at the Don Bosco shelter in Sunyani with 13 other rescued children. He attended school for the next six years and we provided for his basic needs: school fees and supplies, uniforms, medical and food. After Matthew turned 20 and aged out of the shelter, he went to live with his mother (his father had passed away) in Kpando to continue his studies.

RTBF provided a micro grant to his mother to support her smoked fish business and farm to enable her to support Matthew's education. We also paid for the family’s National Health Insurance card to enable them to receive free medical care along with quarterly provisions of clothing and foodstuffs to ease her financial burden.

After Matthew completed junior high school, he was placed at the Kpando Technical Institute. He found the formal education to be challenging and decided to learn a trade through the apprenticeship system. He dreamed of learning how to use Plaster of Paris so RTBF enrolled him in an apprenticeship program. Matthew currently lives in Tema with his Plaster of Paris instructor, Philip. RTBF provides for his basic needs (food, clothing, medical, etc.) and his apprenticeship fees.

After just one year, Matthew has excelled in the program and is now the head of all apprentices. Eric recently met with Philip and Matthew to pay for his second year apprenticeship fee and to monitor his progress. Philip said, “Your boy is doing well and very hard working. I wish to work with him after he completes his training.” Matthew has one more year before he will become a Master of Plaster of Paris.

Matthew told Eric, “Daddy, thank you for all the work you are doing and for giving me hope. One day when you build your house, I will be the one to do the Plaster of Paris for your ceiling so that you can always have me in your house. Thank you.” Matthew’s mother later called Eric and prayed for him and RTBF’s donors to express her immense gratitude.

We are grateful to our donors who made Matthew's freedom possible 11 years ago and have given him the support to fulfil his dream of being a Master in Plaster of Paris. When he completes the program next year, he will have a job and be able to support himself, breaking the cycle of poverty afflicting so many. Despite starting school at the age of 14, Matthew has worked hard to overcome his lost childhood and we are proud of his achievements.

Story of Barnabus

Four years ago, Eric Peasah (Founder/Executive Director of RTBF/Africa) discovered Barnabus scavenging in a landfill in Ankwani Doboro in the Eastern Region of Ghana. We encourage you to read Barnabus' story in our July 2020 newsletter, which detailed the hazardous conditions at the landfill where he lived and worked, collecting items to sell to a recycling company in order to earn money for school tuition for him and his younger brother.

Barnabus caught Eric's attention because he was young and wearing a yellow school uniform (shirt). Everything else he wore he found at the landfill. Eric observed broken plastic bags of human excrement and the stench was unbearable. Scavengers use their 'Dracula' hooks to sort through trash and 'claim' items, which can cause injuries.

After Barnabus was rescued, we enrolled him in his former technical school. However, two months later, Covid lockdowns were announced. As schools remained closed, we supported Barnabus so he would not return to the dangerous work at the landfill to earn money.

Barnabus was not used to being idle so he decided he wanted to learn a skill. He discussed this with Eric and since he had aspirations of being a mechanic, RTBF enrolled him in a mechanic skill training program in January 2021 in Accra (capitol of Ghana).

RTBF rented a single room for Barnabus in a suburb of Accra, close to our office and his workplace and bought him a bicycle, which he rides to work daily. He enjoys the work and said, "I want to be one of the best mechanics in the world in years to come." The shop owner/mentor and his co-workers love to work with him. They say Barnabus is a fast learner, very respectful, pays attention and does whatever he is asked to do neatly and properly. Since he is still in the apprenticeship program, we give him a monthly allowance, pay for his medical insurance and utility bills.

Barnabas is a responsible young adult and works very hard. Since his workplace is close to RTBF's office, this enables the staff and Eric to frequently visit him and have periodic meetings with him and his mentor. Barnabus' favorite comment to Eric each time he comes to the office is, “Daddy, I will make you proud." During a recent visit, Barnabus said, "I am so grateful to you and your sponsors. If not for them, I would still be struggling to survive as a waste picker and would be on the dumpster chasing left overs. I will make you people very proud one day.”

RTBF is already very proud of Barnabus' exemplary work ethic and he is well on his way to achieving his dream of being a professional mechanic.

Story of Abigail

After the death of her father, life became difficult for Abigail and her family. She was only seven years old when her paternal uncle promised her mother that he would assume the responsibility for her care. He said he would enroll Abigail in school and give her a better life. With three younger siblings to raise, Abigail's mother agreed and believed she would be well cared for. Unfortunately, the uncle had an ulterior motive.

He brought Abigail to an island on Lake Volta in Yeji, Ghana, and gave her to a fisherman who became her master, in exchange for money and possible future remittances.

Abigail was forced to work beyond her capabilities. Every day she woke up before sunrise and was tasked with scaling and smoking fish. She often choked on the thick heavy smoke as she prepared the fish for market. Sometimes she was ordered to go fishing on the lake when more hands were needed. Her enslavement continued for years as she worked for the fisherman and his wife with no contact with her family.

In 2004, an informant contacted Eric Peasah (Executive Director, RTBF/Africa) about Abigail's plight. Eric rescued Abigail and her dream of being liberated finally came true. After years of hard labor and separation from her family, Abigail's spirit was broken and she had lost any hope of ever seeing her family again.

Through the generosity of RTBF's benefactors, Abigail rigorously pursued her dream of going to school. She brilliantly passed through primary, junior and senior high school levels. She was determined to make up for the lost years of her education. Abigail set ambitious goals for herself, which she successfully attained. Since she aspired to be a nurse, RTBF funded her enrollment in the Nursing Training College, a four year intensive nursing program.

When Abigail recently visited Eric at RTBF's office, she recalled the years she worked as a slave girl and said, "I never thought I would become a nurse. I would have been married to one of the boys in the village without a life. But because of you Daddy, I am now a nurse. I am now the hope for my family and I help support my siblings and mother. You and RTBF gave me this opportunity. My story can never be told without mentioning your names. May God bless you."

Abigail is currently working as an OR (operating room) nurse in a government hospital in the Oti region of Ghana. She is saving lives and proud of her achievements. She hopes to save enough funds to pursue a master’s degree in health care so she can continue to give back to society.
